Takeaway:  
people's intuition about self-play is basically overfit to GO and chess kinds of two-player zero-sum perfect information games. It turns out that when going out of these games, a lot of the nice properties go away, and self-play becomes much more difficult.

Who's the better poker player?  
- option 1 (minimax equilibrium): someone who wins head-to-head vs. any other play over a large enough sample size  
- option 2 (population Best Response): someone who makes more money playing poker than anyone else

In AI for games, by "solving a game", people typically mean computing a minimax equilibrium. It's a strong assumption and not necessarily what we want. In games like chess and GO, this is fine because it ends up being the same thing; but in other games like poker, we will see that this becomes a very significant problem beyond two-player zero-sum games. It's possible that what we really want is a population best response.

### Minimax Equilibrium

A minimax equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally deviating to a different strategy.
- **Worst-Case Guarantee**: In two-player zero-sum games, playing a minimax equilibrium guarantees that an agent **will not lose in expectation** against any opponent, regardless of how strong they are.
- **Exploitability**: Exploitability measures how much an agent loses to an opponent’s best response. At a minimax equilibrium, exploitability is **zero**.

Equilibrium analysis assumes that the opponent has **full knowledge of your strategy** (such as full access to an AI model’s weights and action probability distributions), but does not know the private outcomes of random processes (the random seed). Speaker highlights that while this is a very strong assumption, it is necessary when building systems deployed to billions of users, where adversaries will eventually discover and exploit any latent structural weakness.

- In simple games like rock-paper-scissors, the equilibrium strategy only guarantees a tie. 
- In complex games such as poker, however, opponents inevitably choose actions that carry **negative expected value**. Because the game is zero-sum, any loss of value caused by an opponent’s mistakes directly translates into profit for the player executing the minimax equilibrium. Modern poker professionals therefore aim to play the equilibrium as their default baseline, allowing opponent errors to generate profit.
